Step 1: the maze

The first step builds the world of the game: a maze of 48 × 48 cells, generated from a seed, about 4700 nodes. A camera flies over it, and a top view in the corner shows what the camera retains: only that is drawn.

…

Click the maze, then: W A S D or the arrows to move, Q and E to turn, the wheel to climb or descend, Shift to go faster. With a gamepad: the left stick to move (pressed to go faster), the bumpers to turn, the pad up and down to climb or descend.

1. Generating the maze

Section titled “1. Generating the maze”

A maze is a grid of cells, each with four walls. The generator walks the grid depth first — the “recursive backtracker”: from a cell, it goes to a random neighbor not visited yet and opens the wall between them; when no neighbor is left, it goes back. Every cell is then reachable, by a single path.

The walls are bits of a Uint8Array: compact, and quick to test. The random generator is seeded (seededRandom(), a mulberry32): the same seed gives the same maze, which matters for tests and for sharing a level.

2. Building the level

Section titled “2. Building the level”

Each cell becomes a floor tile; each closed side, a wall. Both are cubes, scaled:

Three choices matter here:

  • Static nodes. The level never moves: static: true tells the scene to compute these nodes once, and to index them all at once in a spatial index of their own (an octree built top down), at the first frame.
  • Occluders. The walls hide what is behind them: occluder: true marks them. Seen from above, a maze hides little: the game draws it without the occlusion culling, which pays off at the level of the streets.
  • Groups. floor and walls are nodes without mesh: they organize the hierarchy (level.walls.children lists the walls) without costing anything to draw.

3. The keyboard, the mouse and the gamepad

Section titled “3. The keyboard, the mouse and the gamepad”

The game reads its input with @fdw/engine-input. Where the events come from is given by the page or the application — a WebInputSource over the canvas, a NativeInputSource over a native window — and the game builds an Input on it:

The code of the game does not read keys: it reads actions, declared once with their bindings — the keyboard, and the gamepad:

  • KeyW is a position on the keyboard, not a letter: on an AZERTY keyboard, the same key is Z, and the game is played with ZQSD without configuration.
  • move is a vector, x to the right and y forward, of length 0 to 1: four keys, or a stick. The strongest binding wins.

Each frame starts with input.update(): it applies the events received since the previous frame and reads the gamepads, then the state stays the same for the frame. Moving while a key is down is then a matter of multiplying a speed by the time of the frame.

See Input for the whole API.

4. The camera

Section titled “4. The camera”

The camera is a node of the scene: setCamera(camera) makes it follow the node, looking along its −z axis. The fly camera moves the node:

The rotation is a yaw (around the vertical) then a pitch (downwards): the product of two quaternions. The speed grows with the height, so that the maze scrolls at the same pace whatever the zoom.

5. Rendering: the main view

Section titled “5. Rendering: the main view”

renderer.render({ source: scene, … }) would be enough for one view. The top view needs a second one in the same frame, so the frame is a render graph:

The renderer asks the scene what is in the frustum of the camera: of the 4700 nodes, a few hundred. The line under the canvas shows how many, and how many are drawn into the shadow map of the sun — only those whose shadows can fall in view.

6. The top view

Section titled “6. The top view”

The top view draws the level from above, with an orthographic camera, but only the nodes the main camera retained. The scene remembers, on each node, the last camera query that kept it (NodeState.lastVisible); the top view gives the renderer a source that filters on it:

The top view is drawn into a small texture, then over the main view by a full screen pass that only covers a corner of the screen: a material reading the texture in a rectangle, transparent elsewhere, blended (blend: true):

The marker of the camera is a sphere on a layer of its own (TopViewLayer): the main view, whose layers are ~TopViewLayer, does not see it.

What we have

Section titled “What we have”
  • A world of thousands of static nodes, indexed once, and drawn only where the camera looks.
  • A frame made of two views in a render graph, composited with a blended full screen pass.
  • Input that works the same in a page, in the demo and in a native window.
