Step 4: the game

The maze becomes a game. Eight gems are hidden in its dead ends; once they are all collected, the exit opens, in the far corner. Enemies wander the corridors: when they see the character, they chase it, and touching it ends the game. The flashlight is both a help and a danger: it finds the gems and dazzles the enemies, but it is seen from afar. A map shows what was discovered, the rest stays in the fog.

…

Click the maze, then: W A S D to walk, the mouse to aim, F for the flashlight, M to switch the map between the surroundings and the whole maze, R to play again. C shows the top view of the culling of the previous steps instead of the map. With a gamepad: the left stick to walk, the right stick to aim, the right trigger for the flashlight, Back for the map, Start to play again.

1. The logic at a fixed step

Section titled “1. The logic at a fixed step”

Until now, each frame moved the character by the time of the frame. The game logic now runs by fixed steps of 1/60 s, as many as the frame holds:

  • The game is the same at 30 or at 144 frames per second: an enemy covers the same distance per step, a collision is tested at the same places.
  • After a long frame (a tab in the background), at most five steps run: the game slows down rather than running hundreds of steps at once.
  • 1e-9: in floating point, 0.05 + 0.05 is a hair less than 0.1. Without this tolerance, a step would be lost, then two would run in the next frame.

The rendering stays at the rate of the screen: the camera follows the character every frame. A scheduler runs the frame: systems in four phases — begin once, fixed at each step, update once, render last — in the order of their constraints:

  • paused stops the fixed phase only: once caught, the enemies and the player freeze, while the camera, the map and the rendering go on. R calls game.reset(), then scheduler.paused = false.
  • Paused by the game system, the steps left in the frame are dropped: nothing moves after the catch.
  • The input is read in begin, once per frame, before the steps: a press is seen once, even in a frame of two steps.

The tutorial Systems and the fixed step details the scheduler: the order, the pause, drawing between two steps.

2. Gems and enemies: components of the game

Section titled “2. Gems and enemies: components of the game”

Steps 2 and 3 used a component without fields, Solid. The game defines two more, with columns:

Each field is a typed array, indexed by entity: Enemy.state[entity] reads the state of an enemy. A ref column holds any JavaScript value.

The entities are built with nodes — a gem is a node with a mesh, an enemy a node with a body and two eyes — and the component is given to the node:

The node façade builds; the columns compute. Both are views of the same entities.

3. Systems

Section titled “3. Systems”

A system is a function that goes through the entities of a component, and reads and writes its columns. The game has five, run in order at each step:

These systems of the game are methods, called in this order by the game system of the scheduler. Each could be a system of the scheduler of its own ({ phase: 'fixed', after: ['walk'] }); grouped, they share the timer of the sight and the state of the game.

4. Walking through the maze

Section titled “4. Walking through the maze”

The enemies do not collide with walls: they walk the graph of the maze, from the center of a cell to the center of an open neighbor. When they arrive, they choose the next cell:

  • Wandering, an enemy takes a random open neighbor, but not the cell it comes from, unless it is in a dead end: it goes along the corridors instead of shaking on the spot.
  • Chasing, it takes the first step of the shortest path to where it last saw the player. nextStep walks the maze breadth first from the goal, then picks the neighbor closest to it:

The same walk places the game: the exit is the cell farthest from the start, gems go in dead ends at least twelve steps away, enemies twenty.

5. Lines of sight

Section titled “5. Lines of sight”

An enemy sees the player when nothing solid stands between them. A ray of the scene answers that, from the eyes of the enemy to the player:

The ray goes through the static octree: a few walls are tested, not 2400. The hits come nearest first; the first solid one stops the walk.

  • In the dark, an enemy sees the player four units away; with the flashlight on, fourteen.
  • Out of sight, a chasing enemy goes on to the last place it saw the player, then gives up after four seconds.
  • Sight runs ten times per second, not at every step: a few rays per enemy and per second are enough for a reaction, and cost nothing.

The beam dazzles. An enemy in the cone of the beam, close enough and in its line of sight, stops for two seconds, and cannot catch anyone:

inCone compares the angle between the axis of the spot and the enemy with the outer cutoff of the light: the dazzle follows the beam the player sees.

Before reading the position of the light, the system calls scene.flush(): a light follows its node when the scene applies its pending changes, and the character moved during the step.

6. Eyes in the dark

Section titled “6. Eyes in the dark”

The bodies of the enemies are lit: in the dark, they are black. Their eyes are not:

A surface without a shading model is unlit: its color does not depend on the lights. With cullUnlit, such items are always drawn — a light reaching them or not — so the eyes glow wherever an enemy is, a warning before the beam finds it. Dazzled, they turn white: the eye nodes get another material. The exit pad is unlit too, red while gems are left, green once it opens.

7. The map and its fog

Section titled “7. The map and its fog”

The map shows the cells the player discovered. A cell is discovered when the player walks through it or next to it, or when the beam lights it:

The same two tests as the dazzle: in the cone, and in sight. Only the cells not discovered yet, around the light, are tested, ten times per second.

The picture of the map is drawn on the CPU: four pixels per cell, a line for the walls, the floor in between; fog elsewhere. 48 × 48 cells give a picture of 193 × 193 pixels, one 32-bit word per pixel:

It is redrawn only when the exploration changes, and written into a texture with writeTexture:

Drawing the discovered nodes from above, as the top view of the culling does, would cost a draw per node: up to 4700 once the maze is explored. The picture costs one draw, however much is discovered.

A fullscreen pass shows it in the corner. Its material reads a window of the picture, which follows the player (16 cells across), or the whole maze with M; the player is drawn on top, a dot and a smaller one ahead:

The player moves at every frame: it is a uniform, not a pixel of the picture, so the texture does not change for it. The gems and the exit, once their cells are discovered, are marks drawn into the picture.

8. Playing with a gamepad

Section titled “8. Playing with a gamepad”

Nothing of the game knows the gamepad: it reads actions, and the actions of maze/controls.ts bind the gamepad as well as the keyboard. A gamepad plugged in plays at once, in a page or in a native window:

Action Keyboard, mouse Gamepad
move WASD, arrows Left stick
run Shift Left stick pressed
aim The mouse Right stick
turn Q, E Bumpers
zoom Wheel (zoomStep) Pad up and down
flashlight F Right trigger, X (west)
map M Back
restart R Start

The aim follows the device used last: moving the mouse aims at the pointer, pushing the right stick aims in its direction (see step 3). The zoom is two actions: the wheel turns by notches, once, while the pad is held — a speed, multiplied by the time of the frame.

What we have

Section titled “What we have”
  • A scheduler: game logic at a fixed step, the rendering at the rate of the screen, a pause when the game ends.
  • Gems and enemies as entities, built with nodes, updated by systems over the columns of their components.
  • Enemies walking the graph of the maze, and chasing by its shortest paths.
  • Lines of sight with rays of the scene, through its octree.
  • Unlit materials, seen in the dark.
  • A map revealed by exploration, drawn on the CPU and uploaded only when it changes.
  • The keyboard, the mouse and the gamepad, through the same actions.

Some ideas left for later: walls between the camera and the character becoming transparent, and the character drawn between two steps (see Systems and the fixed step).
