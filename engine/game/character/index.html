Step 2: the character

The maze gets a character. It walks with the keyboard, slides along the walls instead of going through them, and the camera follows it from above. Close to the ground, the camera sees a few dozen nodes of the 4700.

…

Click the maze, then: W A S D or the arrows to walk, Shift to run, Q and E to turn the camera around the character, the wheel to bring it closer or further. With a gamepad: the left stick to walk (pressed to run), the bumpers to turn, the pad up and down to zoom.

1. Sharing the world between the steps

Section titled “1. Sharing the world between the steps”

Step 1 built the level and the sun, and rendered the main view and the top view. Step 2 needs both: they move to two modules, used by every step.

A step is then only what it adds:

2. A character made of nodes

Section titled “2. A character made of nodes”

The character is a node, which moves and turns, with two children: a body (a sphere, stretched) and a nose (a small cube in front), which shows where it faces.

The game only moves and turns this.node: the children follow, placed in its space. The nose is at −z, the direction the character faces when it is not turned.

3. What is solid: a component of the game

Section titled “3. What is solid: a component of the game”

The character must not walk through the walls. Which nodes are walls is the game’s business, not the engine’s: the game defines a component in the ECS of the scene, and gives it to the walls.

A component without fields is a tag: Solid.has(entity) tells whether an entity has it. Later, enemies or doors can be solid too, without the collision code changing.

4. Colliding with the walls

Section titled “4. Colliding with the walls”

Each frame, the character moves, then gets out of the walls it overlaps. Seen from above, it is a circle; a wall, a rectangle. The scene finds the walls near it with a query:

  • The query goes through the static octree of the scene: a handful of walls are tested, not 2400.
  • The circle is pushed along the shortest way out: perpendicular to the wall. Its movement along the wall is kept, so the character slides along it instead of stopping dead.
  • In a corner, leaving a wall can push into the other one: the code makes up to three passes.

5. Walking relative to the camera

Section titled “5. Walking relative to the camera”

W, or the left stick pushed up, goes away from the camera, whatever the camera’s direction: the move action is turned by the yaw of the camera before moving the character.

The action is of length 0 to 1: four keys are normalized, so that the diagonal is not faster, and a stick half pushed walks half as fast. The heading follows the movement smoothly; atan2(sin, cos) brings the difference between −π and π, so that the character never turns the long way round.

6. A camera that follows

Section titled “6. A camera that follows”

The camera stays behind and above the character, at a distance, and catches up with it smoothly:

1 − e^(−k·dt) is the right fraction for a frame of any length: two frames of 8 ms move the camera as much as one of 16 ms. The camera looks down steeply (72°): a wall then hides little behind it.

What we have

Section titled “What we have”
  • A character built from nodes, moved and turned as a whole.
  • Collisions found by a query of the scene, filtered by a component of the game, and resolved so that the character slides along the walls.
  • A camera following it, close enough that a few dozen nodes are drawn.

When the character stands just behind a wall, on the side of the camera, the wall still hides it: turning the camera (Q, E) shows it again. Making the walls between the camera and the character transparent is a classic answer, for a later step.

The next step turns the lights off, and gives the character a flashlight.
