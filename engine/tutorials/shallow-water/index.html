Water on a ground

@fdw/engine-fluids simulates fluids on the GPU, in compute graphs, on every backend. It has three methods, each for its kind of fluid:

  • a field of heights (this tutorial): the water of a pond, a river, a pool, over a ground of any shape;
  • a grid of cells: smoke and fire;
  • particles: water that splashes.

The field of heights is the cheapest: one value of water per column, a few thousand columns. It cannot make water fall over itself (a breaking wave, a waterfall), but it fills basins, runs down slopes, overflows, and carries ripples.

Click to drop water; the spring on the island runs down into the pool.

A field of heights

Section titled “A field of heights”

Each cell is a column of water standing on its ground. Its state is in two storage buffers of vec4:

  • the cells: the depth of water, the occupancy (the bodies in the column, see below), the velocity of the water (x and z);
  • the fluxes: the water leaving toward each of the four neighbors, in m³/s.
Option Default What it does
resolution — The cells along x and z
cell 0.25 The side of a cell, in meters
origin centered on 0 The corner of the grid in the world
ground 0 The height of the ground: per cell, a function of the position, or one height
level none Fills the cells up to this height at the start (and at reset())
edges 'closed' closed: walls around the grid; open: the water leaves by the edges
damping 0.2 How fast the waves fade, per second
maxDepth 2 The deepest water expected: the steps are short enough for its waves
stiffness 6 How much of the water a body pushes aside: k / (k + 1)
readback true Reads the water back for the physics, a frame late

Pipes between the cells

Section titled “Pipes between the cells”

The water moves through virtual pipes between neighboring cells (O’Brien and Hodgins, Mei et al.). Each step is two dispatches:

  1. The fluxes. The surface of a cell is H = ground + depth (+ the bodies). The flux toward a neighbor grows with the difference of their surfaces, dt · g · cell · (H − Hneighbor), and never goes negative. Then all the fluxes of a cell are scaled down so that it gives no more water than it has: a cell can dry, never go below zero.
  2. The depths. Each cell gains what its neighbors send it and loses what it sends: d += dt · (in − out) / cell². The velocity of the water comes from the fluxes through the cell.

Nothing is created nor lost: the water of the grid is the same from a step to the next (a test checks it), but for the sources and the open edges.

The steps. The waves of shallow water run at √(g d): over 1.5 m of water, 3.8 m/s. A step must not let them cross more than a cell: update(dt) splits the frame into as many steps as maxStep (half the time a wave takes to cross a cell at maxDepth) asks. With cells of 12.5 cm on 1.5 m of water, that is 2 steps a frame at 60 Hz.

The GPU and the CPU run the same steps (stepShallowWater): the rendering tests check that they agree, on every backend.

Sources and splashes

Section titled “Sources and splashes”

A source pours its rate over the cells whose centers are in its disc, the same depth in each: the volume is exact whatever the size of the cells.

Drawing the water

Section titled “Drawing the water”

After its steps, update writes a third buffer for the rendering: the height of the surface, its slopes and the speed of the water in each cell. The WaterSurface is a grid over the cells, moved up to the water by the material of the sea of the ocean: the same reflection of the sky, the same sun, the same ground seen through the water and absorbed with its depth. The OceanRenderer draws any such WaterItem over the scene drawn first.

  • The shores: the dry cells are discarded (the material is masked), the edge of the water halfway between a wet center and a dry one; their vertices go a little under the ground, so that the water cuts the terrain instead of flickering on it.
  • The foam: where the water runs fast (from foamSpeed, 2 m/s), the material sees squeezed waves, and foams. The stream of the basin below is white where it runs down the hill.
  • The look: fresh water by default (freshWaterLook), clearer than the sea; setLook takes any uniform of WaterLook.

What floats on it

Section titled “What floats on it”

…

Drag to turn around, the wheel to zoom, WASD to move; Space drops a crate, B a ball, E a splash, F stops or starts the spring, R starts again.

Bodies and water act on each other, both ways:

The bodies push the water. FluidBodies writes the spheres and boxes of the bodies (their colliders, or a shape given to add) into a storage buffer, six vec4 each, that the graphs of all the fluids read. Here, each column measures the part of each body under its water: that occupancy raises the surface of the column, and the fluxes push the water away toward the neighbors. A crate dropped in the pond makes a wave; a floating one pushes aside the water it displaces. Not all of it: a column whose surface would follow the body exactly has no stable state (it rings like a U-tube); stiffness (6) pushes 6 / 7 of it aside, stably.

The water carries the bodies. heightAt(x, z) and velocityAt(x, z) read the cells back from the GPU, one frame late. The Buoyancy of the ocean takes anything with heightAt; with velocityAt, the water also drags each wet point toward its velocity: a crate in the stream goes down with it.
