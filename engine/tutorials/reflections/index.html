Reflections

A reflection shows, at each point of a surface, what is seen in the direction of the view bounced off the surface. Following these rays through the scene is what a ray tracer does; a rasterizer finds the same colors in other ways, each with its limits:

Technique Reflects Cost Limits
Environment map a fixed surrounding (sky, room) one read not the objects of the scene
Planar mirror the whole scene the scene drawn a second time one flat surface per extra rendering
Screen space reflections what is on the screen a full screen pass, a read per step nothing that is off the screen or hidden

In the example, chrome balls reflect an environment map, sharp to blurry as their roughness grows. Click to switch the floor between a matte floor, a planar mirror and screen space reflections.

Every technique starts from the same direction: the view, from the camera to the point, reflected by the normal of the surface. std.reflect gives it:

Environment maps

Section titled “Environment maps”

A cube texture holds what is seen in every direction from a point: six square faces, the sides of a cube around it (+x, -x, +y, -y, +z, -z). std.textureSampleCube reads it in a direction, of any length; std.textureSampleCubeLevel at a level of its mipmaps.

The example computes its faces in TypeScript: for each texel, its direction, then the color of a sky or of a checkered ground in that direction. The direction of a texel at (u, v) (from -1 to 1, v down) of each face is the same on every backend:

Face Direction Face Direction
+x (1, -v, -u) -x (-1, -v, u)
+y (u, 1, v) -y (u, -1, -v)
+z (u, -v, 1) -z (-u, -v, -1)

The mipmaps of the cube texture are computed from its first level (mipLevelCount). Each level averages the previous one: the higher the level, the blurrier the environment. A rough surface scatters its reflection: it reads a higher level.

An average of the levels is an approximation of the blur of a rough surface: the exact one (a convolution by the lobe of the material, the image based lighting of the PBR model) will come with the IBL of phase P2.

The environment is the same for every point: the balls do not reflect each other, nor the boxes. It suits a far surrounding (a sky, a landscape) or a probe captured at the center of a room.

The sky

Section titled “The sky”

The background of the example is the same texture, read in the direction of each pixel. A full screen pass gets it from the camera: camera.positionFromDepth gives a point of the ray of the pixel (at any depth), minus the position of the camera.

Planar mirrors

Section titled “Planar mirrors”

A flat mirror reflects the scene as a camera placed behind the mirror, symmetric of the real one, would see it. The scene is drawn a second time, from this camera, into a texture; the mirror shows this texture.

For the floor, the plane y = 0, the mirrored camera has the opposite y, and its rotation the opposite x and z:

A symmetry is not a rotation: no camera sees the mirror image itself. The mirrored camera sees it upside down, which the mirror corrects when it reads it.

The mirror image

Section titled “The mirror image”

The mirrored camera draws the objects, not the mirror, into a texture of the render graph:

Give the passes their own name: each name has its own camera in the frame uniforms. A full screen pass then adds the sky behind the objects, as for the main view (the example writes the depths for it).

Reading it

Section titled “Reading it”

The material of the floor reads the mirror image at the position of its pixel on the screen, which it computes from its world position and the view projection of the camera. The y of the screen points down; the image is upside down: both cancel out.

The mirror image is a texture of the render graph: the scene passes bind it to the materials with inputs, by the name of the texture in their graphs. The graph then knows that the main passes read it, and draws the mirror first.

What to know:

  • The cost: the scene is drawn twice. A smaller mirror texture (size: { relative: 0.5 }) saves most of it, for a blurrier reflection.
  • One plane: each mirror plane needs its own rendering.
  • What is below the mirror would appear in it: here nothing is below the floor. Otherwise, leave these objects out of the mirror passes.
  • The mirror itself must not be drawn by its own passes.

Screen space reflections

Section titled “Screen space reflections”

Screen space reflections (SSR) need no second rendering: they search the reflection in the image already drawn. A full screen pass follows, for each reflective pixel, the reflected ray in the scene, step by step; it projects each point on the screen, and compares its depth with the depths of the objects there. The first point inside an object is where the ray meets the scene: the color of the image there is the reflection.

The pass needs, for each pixel, its position and its normal (the scene outputs), and which surfaces reflect, and how much. The materials write it themselves, with g.output: an output of value 'material' of the scene passes collects it, 0 for the others.

The image has one depth per pixel, the one of the front faces: behind it, nothing says whether there is an object or empty space. A ray passing behind a ball would be taken for a hit, and the ball would smear over what is behind it. The example draws a second depth, the one of the back faces (faces: 'back': the far side of the objects): between the two, the ray is inside an object.

The pass rebuilds the position of the pixel from its depth, and reflects the view:

Then the steps. The material graph has no loops: a TypeScript loop writes the 40 steps into the graph. Each step projects its point with the view projection of the camera, and reads the two depths there:

The first hit is chosen from the last step to the first, with std.select. It lies between the step before (outside) and this step (inside): six halvings put it on the surface, not on a step, which would draw bands, one per step. Where the ray meets nothing on the screen, the example falls back on the environment map: the floor reflects the sky.

What to know:

  • Only what is on the screen can be reflected. An object behind the camera, the back of a box, a box hidden behind a ball: they are not in the image. Seen past a ball, the reflection of the box behind it is cut by the ball. Reflections vanish at the edges of the screen. A fallback (the environment map) hides most of it.
  • The hidden sides: a ray that meets the underside of a ball takes the color of its visible side, the only one in the image. The top of the reflections of the balls is approximate.
  • The back faces give the thickness of closed objects. An open surface (a plane, a leaf) has no back face of its own: give it a thickness, or draw it double-sided. Two objects one behind the other: only the nearer one is known.
  • The steps: their length and their count set the reach (here 40 × 0.1 units). An object thinner than a step can be stepped over.
  • The cost: a second depth of the scene, and two reads per step for every pixel: compute the reflections at half the size, only where the reflectivity is not 0.

Which one?

Section titled “Which one?”
  • Environment maps everywhere, as the fallback of the others: cheap, blurred by the roughness, but blind to the objects.
  • Planar mirrors for a large flat mirror, still water, a polished floor that matters: exact, at the cost of a second rendering.
  • Screen space reflections for wet or glossy floors and walls, many reflective surfaces at once: a fixed cost, with the gaps of the screen.

Ray tracing (phase P10) will reflect what none of them can.
