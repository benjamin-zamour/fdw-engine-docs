Surface details

A brick wall modeled brick by brick would take thousands of triangles. Four techniques give the same impression with a single flat surface and a few textures. They cost more and more, and each one fools the eye a little better:

Technique What it changes Cost
Normal mapping the normal, read from a texture one read
Bump mapping the normal, from a height one read, derivatives
Parallax occlusion the uv, along the view a read per step (16)
Displacement the vertices many vertices

The example shows them on the same wall, from the top left: normal, bump, parallax occlusion, displacement. A lamp turns over them. Click to see the flat wall.

…

All four are macros of the standard library (stdMacros): nodes that the compiler replaces by the nodes they stand for. They return a normal, a uv or an offset, given to the surface.

The textures

Section titled “The textures”

The example computes its three textures in TypeScript:

  • the height map: 1 on the bricks, 0 in the mortar, with beveled edges;
  • the normal map, derived from it: the normal of the relief at each texel, encoded from [-1, 1] to [0, 1];
  • the colors of the bricks and of the mortar.

The textures of a modeling tool work the same. Normal maps follow the OpenGL convention (green up, as in glTF): x along u, y towards the top of the image, z out of the surface. A map in the DirectX convention (green down) needs its green channel inverted.

Every material reads the textures through a uv computed per fragment (std.perFragment): the textures are read for each pixel, not interpolated from the vertices.

Normal mapping

Section titled “Normal mapping”

The normal map gives, for each texel, a normal in the tangent frame of the surface: the tangent (along u), the bitangent (up in the image) and the normal. macro.normalMapping turns it into a normal of the world.

The tangent frame comes from the tangents of the mesh when it has them (the tangent attribute). computeTangents adds them to a mesh with positions, normals and uvs:

Without tangents, the macro computes the frame per pixel from the screen space derivatives of the position and of the uv (its uv input, else the uv of the mesh): no preparation, a little more work for the GPU, and a frame that changes slightly between triangles. Without tangents nor uv, the map is ignored.

strength (1 by default) scales the relief.

Bump mapping

Section titled “Bump mapping”

Bump mapping only needs a height, any value computed per pixel: a texture, a noise, a function of the position. The macro measures how fast it changes from a pixel to the next one, with the derivatives std.dpdx and std.dpdy, and tilts the normal by these slopes (Mikkelsen’s method, 2010). No tangents, no normal map.

The result depends on the resolution of the height: a height map of low resolution, magnified, shows its texels as facets. The derivatives are computed by groups of 2 × 2 pixels: the details smaller than two pixels flicker. Use the mipmaps of the height map (mipLevelCount).

The derivatives

Section titled “The derivatives”

std.dpdx and std.dpdy are available to every material: the change of a value from a pixel to the next one, to the right and below (y points down, as screen-uv, on every backend). std.fwidth is the sum of their absolute values: the width of a pixel in the units of the value, for antialiased procedural patterns.

They only exist in the fragment stage: the compiler refuses a value derived in the vertex stage.

Parallax occlusion mapping

Section titled “Parallax occlusion mapping”

The two previous techniques change the light, not the shape: seen at a grazing angle, the wall stays flat. Parallax mapping moves the uv along the view: a deep point is seen further away than the point of the surface in front of it. macro.parallaxOcclusionMapping follows the ray of the view under the surface, step by step, until it meets the relief of the height map: the bricks hide the mortar behind them.

The material graph has no loops: the macro writes its steps reads one after the other, at compilation (8 to 32; 16 by default). More steps, fewer layering artifacts at grazing angles, more reads. Between the last two steps, the macro interpolates the point where the ray meets the relief.

macro.parallaxMapping, with the same inputs, takes a single read: a cheaper shift, convincing for shallow reliefs, without occlusion.

The silhouette of the surface does not change: the edges of the wall stay straight. The tangent frame comes, as for normal mapping, from the tangents or from the derivatives.

Displacement mapping

Section titled “Displacement mapping”

Displacement mapping moves the vertices themselves, along their normal, by the height map: the silhouette changes, the relief casts real shadows. It needs a mesh with enough vertices: createPlane(180) splits the plane in 180 × 180 cells. Neither WebGPU nor WebGL2 has hardware tessellation: the adaptive version, patches subdivided by their size on the screen, is in the Tessellation tutorial.

The macro reads the height in the vertex stage, with std.textureSampleLevel at the level 0: std.textureSample needs the derivatives of the fragment stage.

The stage of a read

Section titled “The stage of a read”

The compiler places each node in the stage where its inputs are: a texture read from the uv attribute, with textureSampleLevel, runs in the vertex stage, and its result is interpolated between the vertices. It is what displacement needs, and a trap elsewhere: the texture seems blurred, read only at the vertices. To read it per pixel, pass the uv through std.perFragment first, as the examples here do.

Which one?

Section titled “Which one?”
  • Normal mapping for most surfaces: cheap, precise, from the maps of the modeling tools. Compute the tangents of the meshes once.
  • Bump mapping for procedural details or a height map alone, without preparing anything.
  • Parallax occlusion mapping for deep reliefs seen at an angle (walls, paving), at a cost that grows with the steps; the silhouette stays flat.
  • Displacement for reliefs that change the silhouette (terrains), on meshes subdivided enough.
