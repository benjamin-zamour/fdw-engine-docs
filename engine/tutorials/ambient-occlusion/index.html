Ambient occlusion

The ambient light comes from everywhere at once: it lights a corner as much as an open floor, and objects seem to float. In reality, a corner, a crease or the ground under a ball receives less of it, because the geometry around hides part of the sky. Ambient occlusion measures, for each point, how much is hidden, and darkens the ambient light by as much.

Screen space ambient occlusion (SSAO) estimates it from what the camera sees: the normals and the depths of the pixels. Click the example to switch between the image with occlusion, without it, and the occlusion alone.

…

The frame has four steps, all in a render graph:

  1. The scene passes draw the lit color and write, alongside it, the normal and the depth of each pixel (the G-buffer).
  2. A full screen pass estimates the occlusion of each pixel from them.
  3. A second one blurs the estimate.
  4. A third one composes the image: the ambient light is darkened, the direct light is left as is.

1. The normals and the depths

Section titled “1. The normals and the depths”

The occlusion pass needs, for each pixel, the normal of the surface and its distance to the camera. Both are values the scene passes can write (see Scene outputs). A function graph puts them in one rgba16float texture, the normal in xyz and the depth in w: the occlusion pass reads them with a single sample.

Both are in view space: the camera at the origin, looking at −z. The depth is the distance along the axis of the camera, positive; 0 where there is no object.

2. The occlusion

Section titled “2. The occlusion”

For each pixel, the pass places 16 points in the half sphere above the surface, oriented by its normal, and counts the ones that are behind the scene: a point hidden by the geometry is a direction from which the ambient light does not come.

The point of the pixel

Section titled “The point of the pixel”

The depth and the position on the screen give the point in view space. With a perspective projection, a point at depth d lands on the screen at x × p / d, where p is the scale of the projection: the inverse gives it back.

The samples

Section titled “The samples”

The 16 points are computed once, in TypeScript: directions of the half sphere around z, at distances from 0.1 to 1, more of them close to the center (the nearby geometry matters most).

A matrix turns them around the normal of the pixel: its columns are a tangent, a bitangent and the normal. With the same 16 directions everywhere, the occlusion would show bands; the tangent is turned by an angle that changes from one pixel to the next, over a tile of 4 × 4 pixels. Neighboring pixels look in different directions, and the blur (step 3) averages them.

Hidden or not

Section titled “Hidden or not”

The graph has no loops: a TypeScript loop writes the 16 samples into it, one after the other. For each one, the point is projected on the screen (the inverse of the reconstruction above) and compared to the depth of the scene there.

Four values set the result:

  • radius (0.5 here, in the units of the scene): how far the geometry hides the ambient light. Small: only the creases darken; large: wide soft shadows around the objects.
  • bias (0.025): a margin on the comparison. Without it, the surface hides its own samples because of the precision of the depths (acne).
  • power (2): the occlusion raised to a power, to strengthen it.
  • near, the range check: a point can be behind an object far in front of the pixel, the edge of a cube in front of the wall. Without it, the wall behind would get a dark halo around the cube.

The G-buffer is read with a nearest sampler: a linear one would blend, at the edges of the objects, the depth of the object with the depth of what is behind it, a depth that does not exist. Its wrap mode is clamp-to-edge: a point that projects off the screen must not read the other side of it.

The occlusion is written into an r8unorm texture of the graph: one value per pixel, from 0 to 1.

3. The blur

Section titled “3. The blur”

Each pixel has looked in 16 directions only, and its neighbors in others: the raw occlusion shows the 4 × 4 tile. The average of each block of 4 × 4 pixels removes it, since it contains the 16 angles.

16 reads are not needed: a linear sampler, read exactly between four pixels, returns their average. Four reads, at −½ and +1½ pixel, cover the 16.

The size of a pixel changes with the canvas: it is set every frame.

Section titled “4. The composition”

What to know

Section titled “What to know”
  • Only what is on the screen counts. An object outside the image, or hidden behind another, does not darken anything; occlusion appears and disappears at the edges of the screen when the camera moves.
  • The cost is 16 reads per pixel. To save it: compute the occlusion at half the size (size: { relative: 0.5 } of createTexture) and blur it at full size, or take 8 samples.
  • The blur crosses the edges: the occlusion of a wall bleeds a little onto the object in front of it. A blur that compares the depths (bilateral) avoids it, for 16 reads.
  • The composition depends on the shading model. It removes ambient × baseColor, the ambient term of phong; with several lights, add their ambient. Deferred rendering will apply the occlusion directly where the ambient light is computed.
  • The PBR model already multiplies its ambient term by the occlusion input of the surface: occlusion baked in a texture by a modeling tool, finer and stable, but which does not know about the other objects. Both combine.
