Smoke and fire

Smoke is a gas carried by the air: to draw it, one simulates the air, its velocity in each cell of a grid, and carries the smoke and the heat along. SmokeGrid does it on the GPU, in compute graphs, on every backend, after Stam’s stable fluids: a step stays stable whatever its length.

…

Move the pointer over the canvas: the disc follows it and pushes the smoke.

A grid of cells

Section titled “A grid of cells”

The grid is staggered (a MAC grid, Harlow and Welch): the velocity across each face of the cells, the rest at their centers. The pressure that keeps the air from compressing then balances exactly with the flow through the faces, without the checkerboard a grid of centers leaves (which, among other things, kept the smoke from going around an obstacle).

Option Default What it does
resolution — The cells along x, y, z; z = 1 for a plane
cell 0.1 The side of a cell, in meters
origin centered, on y = 0 The lowest corner of the grid in the world
open all the faces but the floor Where the smoke leaves the box ('+y', '-x'…); others: walls
buoyancy 1 How hard the heat lifts the air, per degree
weight 0.05 How hard the smoke weighs
vorticity 2 The confinement of the swirls
cooling 0.8 How fast the heat goes, per second
densityDecay 0.05 How fast the smoke thins, per second
iterations 20 The steps of the pressure solve
extinction 8 How much a meter of thick smoke dims the light

A step

Section titled “A step”

update(dt) submits a frame in a single submission, a pass per dispatch:

  1. The obstacles: the cells in a body (see below) take its velocity.
  2. The advection: each face traces the velocity back over dt (a midpoint step) and takes the velocity it finds there, read between the faces around it; each cell does the same for the smoke and the heat. Then the forces: the heat lifts (buoyancy × temperature), the smoke weighs; the emitters pour, the smoke thins, the heat goes.
  3. The vorticity: the curl of the velocity, then the confinement, a force that turns the air around the swirls that the advection blurs away (Fedkiw et al.): the volutes of the smoke.
  4. The pressure: the divergence of each cell (the air coming in, minus the air going out), then iterations steps of Jacobi: the pressure that would cancel it. The walls and the obstacles let nothing through; the open faces let the air out.
  5. The projection: each face loses the difference of pressure across it. The air no longer compresses.

The CPU runs the same frame (stepSmoke): the rendering tests check that both agree, in 3D and in a plane, on every backend.

Emitters

Section titled “Emitters”

An emitter is a sphere, full in its inner half and fading to its surface. It pours density per second, heats up to temperature (above the air: 0 is the ambient air), and with velocity, blows. turbulence makes it flicker: a noise rising through it takes up to that share of its smoke and heat, the tongues of a flame.

Obstacles

Section titled “Obstacles”

The cells whose centers are in a body are solid, and move with it: the air does not cross the faces next to them, but slides along. A ball moving through the smoke drags it.

Section titled “In a volume”

…

Click to switch between the fire and the chimney.

The SmokeRenderer draws the scene first, in linear colors, with its view depth; then a full screen pass marches through the grid for each pixel: along the ray of the camera, from where it enters the box of the grid to where it leaves it or meets the scene (an obstacle inside the smoke cuts it), at steps steps (64). Each step:

  • reads the shade of the grid between the eight cells around: the density, the light of the sun that reached the cell, the temperature;
  • dims what is behind by the smoke of the step (Beer-Lambert);
  • adds the light the smoke scatters toward the camera: the sun (a Henyey-Greenstein phase, forward) and the sky;
  • adds the light of the fire: hot cells glow, deep red, then orange (fireColor, fireThreshold).

The light in the smoke is computed once per cell, after each update: a march from each cell toward the sun (lightSteps, 24 cells) sums the smoke on the way. The march of the pixels reads it: 8 reads per step instead of a march toward the sun at each one.

The steps start at a different place for each pixel (a noise): a fine grain instead of bands. addPass(graph, { smoke, color, depth }) adds the march to a render graph of one’s own, after other passes (the water of the ocean, for instance).
