Ocean

@fdw/engine-ocean draws a sea on every backend, lit by its environment and the sun, and tells the physics where the water is. The tutorials show it at work: the sea and its waves, floating objects.

Piece What it is
OceanSpectrum The waves of an oceanographic spectrum, computed on the GPU by inverse FFTs, in cascades
GerstnerWaves Sums of Gerstner waves, evaluated in the vertex shader and on the CPU
Ocean The render item: a grid of rings around the camera, its material, the height of the water
OceanRenderer The passes: the scene, the sky, the sea seeing through to the scene, the tone mapping
Buoyancy Archimedes for the bodies of @fdw/engine-physics, at points, with the drag of the water
createSky, skyColor A daylight sky in a cube texture, with its levels: what the water reflects

The waves

Section titled “The waves”

Both sources give the same eight values at each point (x0, z0) of the still surface: its displacement (dx, h, dz) and their derivatives (∂h/∂x, ∂h/∂z, ∂dx/∂x, ∂dz/∂z, ∂dx/∂z), from which the normal and the jacobian of the motion come (WaveSample, sampleNormal, sampleJacobian).

OceanSpectrum (Tessendorf, Simulating Ocean Water):

  • createSpectrum(options) draws, once on the CPU, a random complex amplitude h0(k) for each wave vector of each cascade, of variance S(k) Δk² / 2, S being the spectrum: JONSWAP (wind speed, fetch, peak enhancement γ) or Phillips, spread around the wind by cos^2s(θ/2). The frequencies are those of deep water, ω = √(g|k|) (with depth, √(g|k| tanh(|k|d))), rounded to multiples of 2π / period: the sea loops.
  • update(time) runs four compute graphs: the spectrum at that time, then the stages of the FFT along the rows then the columns (one dispatch per stage, each reading the butterflies of its stage from a storage buffer), then the fields. Two real fields share each complex FFT (a + ib, their spectra being hermitian): four FFTs for eight fields.
  • The cascades are tiles of different sizes (233, 37.7 and 6.3 m by default), each keeping the waves of a band of lengths, up to 4 waves along the side of the next tile. A cascade whose band would reach beyond its shortest wave is refused.
  • displacement and derivatives, two storage buffers of vec4 (cascade, row along z, column along x), are what the material reads.
  • The CPU side: fields (the last displacement read back, or computed by evaluateSpectrum before the first read or with readback: false), sample(x0, z0), heightAt(x, z). The FFT of the CPU (inverseFft2d, butterflyTables) is the one of the GPU, run by the tests against the GPU on WebGPU, Vulkan and WebGL2.

GerstnerWaves: a list of { direction, wavelength, amplitude, steepness, phase }. The horizontal motion of a wave is steepness / (k × count): with every steepness at 1, the crests of all the waves pinch to a point where they meet. The material gets their constants in the code and their phases in uniforms (wavePhases0…, wrapped to [0, 2π) on the CPU: no precision lost after hours).

The grid

Section titled “The grid”

clipmapGrid({ cell, levels, size, horizon }): squares of 2 × size cells, the first of cell meters (0.5), each ring around the previous one with cells twice as large (7 rings: 1 km on each side), then a flat skirt to the horizon (20 km at least), about 23 000 vertices in all. The Ocean moves it under the camera by steps of the coarsest cell (grid.step, 32 m), so that its vertices stay on the same points of the world. On the outer edge of a ring, the vertices between two of the next ring are moved onto one of them: the edges match, without cracks once displaced. The waves fade out of the vertices before the skirt.

The tessellation of P3b was the other choice: its patches are fixed in the world, while the sea goes to the horizon wherever the camera is; the rings follow the camera at no CPU cost.

The material

Section titled “The material”

oceanGraph(shader, { refraction, fade }), on the WaveShader of the waves (spectrumShader, gerstnerShader): a custom one is possible, with the same three methods.

  • Vertex stage: the still point (x0, z0) of the vertex, the waves added, each faded with the distance at fade times its length (the side of its tile for a cascade): beyond, the vertices are too far apart.
  • Fragment stage: the derivatives at the still point of the pixel; the waves shorter than a few pixels fade out, the variance of their slopes added to the GGX roughness (α² + 2σ²): the far water turns into a sheen.
  • Light: the environment reflected (Fresnel of water, F0 = 0.02), its level by the roughness; a GGX highlight of the sun; through the water, the scene bent by the normal, absorbed by e^(−absorption × depth), and the light scattered by the water; the sun through the crests; the foam where the jacobian falls below foamThreshold and where the water is thinner than shoreFoam.
  • The uniforms of WaterLook (setLook), sunDirection and sunColor (setSun(light, intensity)), the environment cube texture and its environmentLevels.

The passes

Section titled “The passes”

OceanRenderer.addPasses(graph, options) (the options of addScenePasses, plus ocean):

Pass Writes Reads
scene the color (rgba16float), the view depth —
ocean background the sky where the depth is 0, twice (MRT) the color, the depth
ocean the sea over one copy, against the scene depth the other copy, the depth (inputs)

It returns the image, in linear colors; render adds the tone mapping (neutral by default) and presents. The backend must render to rgba16float (all of them do; WebGL2 through EXT_color_buffer_float).

ocean is any WaterItem: a render item on the material of oceanGraph with its refraction. The WaterSurface of @fdw/engine-fluids (the water of a field of heights) is one: its WaveShader adds a coverage, the pixels under 0.5 discarded (a masked material), where the ground is dry.

The physics

Section titled “The physics”

ocean.heightAt(x, z) is the height of the water above a point of the world: the point of the still surface that the waves move there is found by six fixed point iterations. For the spectrum, the heights come from the GPU one frame late (buffer.read(), not awaited: the next read starts once the last one is done).

new Buoyancy(water, { density, gravity }), add(body, { points, volume, depth, drag }), install(scheduler) (the fixed step, before physics). Each point stands for volume / count; wet over depth (the cube root of its share by default), it pushes up by ρ g V × how wet it is, and drags against the velocity of the point (v + ω × r) by drag × mass / count. boxPoints spreads the points of a box. When the water has a velocityAt(x, z, out) (the fluids do), the drag is against the velocity of the water: a current carries the bodies.

Costs

Section titled “Costs”

On the coast of the tutorial (1280 × 720, Vulkan, RTX 2060 SUPER, without vsync): 3.3 ms a frame with the spectrum, 1.9 ms with six Gerstner waves (the coast, nine bodies floating, the readback included). The spectrum itself, 128 × 128 points and three cascades: 0.16 ms of GPU per update (0.65 ms at 256); reading back its 768 KB takes 0.9 ms, without waiting for it. WebGL2 runs the FFTs by rendering points to textures: 32 draws of 49 152 points per frame, 0.36 s an update on SwiftShader (the software renderer of the tests).

Limits

Section titled “Limits”
  • No view under the water, no wakes, no foam that lasts (the foam is that of the frame): later.
  • The heights read back are a frame late: a few millimeters, a few centimeters in a storm.
  • Shadows do not fall on the water, nor do lights other than the sun light it.
  • The waves are those of deep water: no refraction of the waves by the sea floor, no breaking on the beach (the foam and the absorption follow the depth, the waves do not).
